# Write and lay out a Field Guide page

Use the same book record for the Windows and browser readers. Start a route where the player can recognise the room or entrance. Put the immediate decision under the place name. Someone holding a controller should be able to find the next turn without reading a paragraph of background.

## Directions and references

- Give the entrance, landmark, action and result. At Cedar Woods, “Leave through the downstairs front door” resolves a choice that “leave the house” misses. Use compass directions only when the map or the game establishes them. Write for the default camera or name the landmark.
- Draw a map when it explains a choice. Give its pins the same numbers as the directions. Use a solid line for the main path and a labelled dashed line for a detour. Include a text route, including exits and elevations. An editorial sketch and a floor read from the owner's disc each need their own label.
- Put a warning immediately before its trigger. At the Dam, identify the last safe action and the actual rewards at stake. Put the flywheel retry advice before the first attempt. Keep what happens after the key exchange under a separate reveal.
- Show each area’s complete recorded items and enemies. Retain quantity, exact name, where to look, how to acquire it and any condition. Distinguish stealing, learning, a guaranteed reward and a possible drop. If the rate is unknown, leave the rate out. Link a named entry back to every known route where the reader can find it.
- Give each master a location, recruitment condition, unit of progress and full reward table. For combos, identify the casting order and result. Keep the games' learning mechanics distinct. III uses Examine. IV uses Guard. Keep acquisition, battle mechanics and character growth in separate columns or sections.
- Keep later story names out of a fresh player's search results, index and related links until the reader chooses to reveal them. A manual bookmark, a visited place and an ordinary save are different records.
- Use direct, original sentences. Cut promotional promises, generic advice and claims about how the page was built. A lore note needs a specific fact and a source. Keep sources and verification details available at the foot of the entry. If an input binding can change, name the action or show the current binding.

## Type, colour and controls

Use Gloock for titles, Barlow Semi Condensed for short labels and a system sans serif for paragraphs and controls. Face numerals and table entries in the same direction as the corresponding header. Wrap descriptions naturally. Use tabular figures for numbers; fit wide comparisons into their own labelled scroller or a stacked row on a phone. Keep text selectable in the browser.

Use the painted highlands above III's blue command panel and the sand-sea painting alongside IV's ink-dark command panel. Leave the dragon's head in the crop. Keep text on a quiet or opaque ground and one primary command in view. Use a retained heading, focus indicator and text for warnings, selected rows and state changes. Colour alone cannot carry that meaning.

Aim for 16–18 logical pixels for body text on desktop and 20–24 in TV mode. Keep a reading line around 45–75 characters on desktop. Use at least 4.5:1 contrast for normal text and 3:1 for large text and focus indicators. Headings, counts, disclosures and buttons must remain visible with long names, 200% text or a remapped control. Text may grow taller.

## Review a change

Open the part a player came to read. A chapter needs an instruction above the fold and one section selector for directions, floors, items, enemies and notes. Omit empty sections. A diagram belongs beside its instruction. If the screen is narrow, keep the selected instruction close to the numbered map. Keep sources and the wider chapter index within reach without making the reader scroll past them.

An item or enemy can appear several times in one chapter. Open at the exact sighting the player selected, including its room or shelf. Keep other accessible sightings below it. Give a reader arriving from a shared link a named route back into the book. When they came through the guide itself, Back restores the originating section, row, floor or map number. Check these journeys with Ammonia in both the passage and the Synesta Cellar, as well as a subject that appears in only one place. Check them in both books, at phone width and with enlarged text.

Identify each village, pass or dungeon by its own stable area ID. Assign floors, treasure and enemies explicitly, including any observation that genuinely belongs in two places. Keep older bookmarks working when a chapter splits. On an illustrated map, put each number beside a landmark a player can see. Check the label and its number at the map's normal reading size. Label a lasting loss as missable only when the return conditions are known.

Use the community walkthroughs to check what the reader is likely to ask. For III, compare the relevant section of DavidK5 with another walkthrough or a specialist author. For IV, compare Dalez with RealXaenith, then use Billy_Lee or a specialist for disputed facts. The GameFAQs indexes distinguish full guides, specialist guides, editions and recommendation badges. Record the displayed edition, author and section. Treat a recommendation as a discovery aid. Confirm the actual route, skill, quantity or timing separately. At each stop, check arrival and departure, party or field action, optional detours, treasure and shops, enemies and learnt skills, boss behaviour, save and healing points, missables and the return visit. Also check unlocks for masters, combos, dragons, fishing, settlements and minigames. Add each useful fact where a player makes the decision. Cite the source. A brief instruction in original words, a named item and a linked reference can do more work than another paragraph. Keep walkthrough prose, diagrams and table arrangements with their authors unless a reuse grant covers the intended use.

1. Check the cited game revision or identify the author and source of the fact. Credit contributors and retain each asset's licence. Keep game frames and disc-derived maps in the private Lab.
2. Use a blank profile and a returning reader. Operate the changed page by pointer, keyboard and controller navigation. Check the first and last rows of each ledger, both directions of a link, the back action, and the spoiler boundary.
3. Open the page in the offline HTML and the running Windows package. Review a phone-width browser, desktop, large text and TV layout. Inspect the actual words at the screen edge, the active pin and the focus target after a dialog or detour closes.
4. For a navigational change, trace the route from the stated entrance. Record what was observed and what still rests on written sources. For an editorial review, ask an unfamiliar reader where to turn and when to collect the missable. Aim for four of five readers finding the answer in twenty seconds.

Automated checks protect stable links, complete tables, matching map pins, spoiler control and a small list of recurring filler phrases. An editor still decides whether a sentence helps a player.
